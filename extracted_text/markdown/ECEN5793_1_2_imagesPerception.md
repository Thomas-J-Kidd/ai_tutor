FIGURE 1.5 The electromagnetic spectrum arranged according to energy per photon.

Major uses of imaging based on Gamma ray include nuclear medicine and astronomical observations.

- Inject a patient with a radioactive isotope that emits gamma-ray as it decays.
- Images are produced from the emissions collected by gamma ray detectors
- Gamma-ray imaging is widely used to diagnose various conditions and examine the structure and function of many different parts of the body, including the heart, brain, lungs, and kidneys

- An imaging radar works like a flash camera in that it provides its own illumination (microwave pulses) to illuminate an area on the ground or an object and take a snapshot.
- A radar uses an antenna and computer to record its image by collecting reflected microwave energy.

# Radio Band Imaging

Energy of one photon (electron volts)
FIGURE 1.5 The electromagnetic spectrum arranged according to energy per photon.

In medicine, radio waves are used in Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

- MRI technique places a patient in a powerful magnet and passes radio waves through his or her body in short pulses.
Each pulse causes a responding pulse of radio waves to be emitted by the patient's tissues.
- Different tissue types with different water densities and thermodynamics are reflected by different energy emitted by the tissue.
- The location from which these signals originate and their strength and determined by a computer.

The principal difference between the lens of the eye and ordinary optical lens is that the former is flexible.

The shape of the lens is controlled by tension in the fibers of the ciliary body.

- When lens are controlled to be flattened, we can focus on distant objects.
- When lens are controlled to become thicker, we can focus on object near the eye.

# Weber's Law

Weber's law: a just noticeable difference in light intensity is a constant proportion of the background intensity.

$$
\frac {| f _ {s} - f _ {0} |}{f _ {s}} = \text{constant} \quad f _ {0} \text{ (luminance of an object)}
$$

$$
\frac {\Delta f}{f} \cong \Delta c \approx 0.02 \quad f _ {s} \text{ (luminance of its surrounding)}
$$

$$
\Delta f \text{ is small for just noticeably different luminance.}
$$

We can determine the different gray levels as follows:

$$
\frac {f _ {k + 1} - f _ {k}}{f _ {k}} = \Delta c \quad \rightarrow f _ {k + 1} = f _ {k} (1 + \Delta c)
$$

- The number of gray levels $N$ is determined by the Contrast Ratio (CR) $\frac{f_N}{f_0}$ (where $f_N$ and $f_0$ are the brightest/darkest gray levels) and the sensitivity ratio $\Delta C$.

# Number of Gray Levels

Contrast ratio (CR) refers to the ratio of intensities between the most intense (brightest, $f_{N} = 1$) and least intense (darkest, $f_{0} = \frac{1}{CR}$) elements of a scene, an image, or a display.

[tbl-1.md](tbl-1.md)

$$
\begin{array}{l}
\begin{array}{l}
\square f_{N} = 1 = \frac{(1 + \Delta c)^{N}}{CR} \rightarrow CR = (1 + \Delta c)^{N} \\
\vdots \\
f_{k+1} = f_{k}(1 + \Delta c) \\
\vdots \\
f_{2} = \frac{(1 + \Delta c)^{2}}{CR} \\
f_{1} = \frac{1 + \Delta c}{CR} \\
f_{0} = \frac{1}{CR} \\
\end{array}
\quad
\text{Number of gray levels:} \\
N = \frac{\log(CR)}{\log(1 + \Delta c)}
$$
